Pulse Width and Duty Cycle

How varying duty cycle changes harmonic content, why cancellation occurs at certain widths, and what PWM modulation actually does

The Square Wave as a Special Case

A square wave is a pulse wave with a 50% duty cycle — it spends equal time high and low. The duty cycle is the ratio of time spent in the high state to the total period:

duty cycle = pulse width / period

A 50% duty cycle gives you a square wave. But the duty cycle can be anywhere from 0% to 100%, and as it moves away from 50%, the harmonic content changes in a specific, predictable way.

Harmonic Content vs. Duty Cycle

The Fourier series for a pulse wave with duty cycle d is:

x(t) = d + Σ [(2/nπ) × sin(nπd) × cos(2πnf₀t)]

The amplitude of the nth harmonic is proportional to sin(nπd) / n. This is the key.

At 50% duty cycle (d = 0.5), sin(nπ × 0.5) = sin(nπ/2). This equals zero for all even n, which is why a square wave contains only odd harmonics.

At 25% duty cycle (d = 0.25), sin(nπ × 0.25) = sin(nπ/4). Now the only zeros occur when nπ/4 is a multiple of π — when n is a multiple of 4. So a 25% pulse wave lacks every 4th harmonic.

In general, a pulse wave with duty cycle 1/k is missing every kth harmonic. A 33% pulse wave is missing every 3rd harmonic (3rd, 6th, 9th…). A 10% pulse wave is missing every 10th harmonic.

Harmonic Cancellation

The cancellation at specific harmonics is the most useful property of pulse width variation. By tuning the duty cycle, you can create notches in the harmonic spectrum at specific multiples of the fundamental.

This is not the same as a comb filter — a comb filter’s notches are spaced in absolute frequency. Pulse width notches track the pitch, staying at the same harmonic ratio regardless of what note you play. The spectral shape is tied to the pitch.

Why Narrow Pulses Sound Thin

As the duty cycle moves toward 0% or 100%, the pulse becomes very narrow or very brief. In this limit, the harmonic amplitudes become increasingly equal — the 1/n rolloff flattens out. At the extreme (a very narrow impulse), all harmonics are present at equal amplitude.

But the DC offset also increases: more time spent high means higher average voltage. In synthesizers, this DC is blocked by capacitors. What remains perceptually is a thin, reedy tone — the narrow pulse emphasizes higher harmonics relative to the fundamental.

Pulse Width Modulation

Pulse width modulation (PWM) means varying the duty cycle over time, typically using an LFO. The result is a slowly animated timbral movement — as the harmonic cancellation notches sweep through the spectrum, the sound takes on a characteristic thick, chorus-like quality.

The chorus-like quality comes from a real physical effect: as harmonics cancel and re-emerge, the balance between them shifts continuously. This sounds similar to two slightly detuned oscillators beating against each other, because the mechanism is similar — the harmonic amplitudes modulate at the LFO rate, producing amplitude modulation of each harmonic at a rate that scales with harmonic number.

At LFO rate f_lfo, the nth harmonic gets amplitude-modulated at f_lfo. This generates sidebands around each harmonic at ±f_lfo. These sidebands are inharmonic with the original series if f_lfo doesn’t divide evenly into f₀, giving a rich cluster of partials.

At 50%: The Richest Minimum

A counter-intuitive result: 50% duty cycle gives the thinnest spectrum despite sounding thick and full. Odd harmonics only — no even harmonics at all. The spectrum looks like every other harmonic is zeroed out.

A 51% duty cycle, however, has all harmonics. The even harmonics are weak (the cancellation isn’t exact) but they’re there. As you move from 50%, even harmonics gradually emerge, and the sound gains a more complex character.

This is why PWM movement centered on 50% is so effective — you’re sweeping through a maximum-cancellation point and moving to either side, causing even harmonics to appear and disappear in the sound.

Physical Analog: Reeds and Bores

The pulse wave’s harmonic behavior has a real-world acoustic analog. Woodwind instruments behave differently depending on whether the bore is cylindrical or conical:

  • Clarinet (cylindrical bore, single reed): overblows at the octave plus fifth, producing odd harmonics only — analogous to a square wave.
  • Saxophone (conical bore, single reed): overblows at the octave, producing all harmonics — more analogous to a sawtooth.

This is not coincidental. The geometry of the bore determines which harmonics can form standing waves, just as the duty cycle determines which harmonics are present in a pulse train.
