Sawtooth Waves and the Fourier Series

The Fourier series made concrete: why sawtooth waves contain all harmonics and how many you need to approximate one

From Theory to Waveform

The previous article established that any periodic waveform can be expressed as a sum of sine waves. The sawtooth wave is the best illustration of this: it has the simplest harmonic series, and the connection between its spectral description and its visual shape is direct and audible.

The Harmonic Series of a Sawtooth

A perfect sawtooth wave with amplitude A and fundamental frequency f₀ has the following Fourier series:

x(t) = (2A/π) × [sin(2πf₀t)/1 - sin(4πf₀t)/2 + sin(6πf₀t)/3 - ...]

Or more compactly:

x(t) = (2A/π) × Σ [(-1)^(n+1) × sin(2πnf₀t) / n]

In plain terms: the nth harmonic has frequency n×f₀ and amplitude 1/n. The sign alternates — fundamental positive, second harmonic negative, third positive, and so on. (The sign matters for the shape but not for the spectrum analyzer, which shows only amplitudes.)

This series never terminates. A theoretically perfect sawtooth requires infinitely many harmonics.

Reconstructing a Sawtooth

What does the ear hear as you add harmonics one at a time?

1 harmonic (fundamental only): Pure sine tone. No resemblance to a sawtooth.

2 harmonics: The second harmonic is added at 1/2 amplitude. The waveform starts to lean — you can see the beginning of an asymmetric shape.

4 harmonics: A distinctly wobbly approximation of the ramp shape. The Fourier approximation overshoots at the discontinuity — this is called Gibbs phenomenon.

8 harmonics: Recognizably sawtooth-shaped. The ramp is clear but the top of the wave still ripples noticeably.

16 harmonics: Clean enough for most purposes. The Gibbs overshoot at the discontinuity persists at roughly 9% of amplitude regardless of how many harmonics are added — it never disappears, only gets narrower.

32+ harmonics: The ripple retreats to the discontinuity edge. Below 20 kHz, a sawtooth at, say, 100 Hz needs 200 harmonics before you run out of audible frequency range.

The Gibbs Phenomenon

That 9% overshoot at the discontinuity deserves attention. No matter how many harmonics you add, the Fourier approximation always overshoots by roughly the same amount at any sharp edge in a waveform. The overshoot gets spatially narrower but does not shrink.

In analog circuitry, real oscillator waveforms have slight rounding at the discontinuity due to slew rate limits in transistors and op-amps. This is not strictly a flaw — it limits how sharp the edge is, which effectively provides some natural high-frequency rolloff. The characteristic brightness of different analog oscillator designs is partly determined by how much this rounding occurs.

Why 1/n Amplitude?

The 1/n amplitude relationship follows directly from what a sawtooth looks like in time. The waveform makes a sharp discontinuous jump — the equivalent of a step function — and it is step functions that require the 1/n series to describe. Smoother shapes (like a triangle wave) have softer discontinuities, and the harmonics fall off faster: 1/n² for triangle.

The faster the harmonic amplitude falls with increasing frequency, the smoother the waveform in time. A sine wave is maximally smooth (no discontinuities of any order) and has exactly one term in its “Fourier series.” A square wave has a sharp edge like a sawtooth, and its odd harmonics also fall at 1/n.

Spectrum as Filter Design

Because you know the sawtooth spectrum precisely, designing synthesis sounds is equivalent to designing a filter. If you want a sound with only the first six harmonics of a sawtooth at equal amplitude, you are designing a comb filter that passes those six frequencies and blocks everything else. If you want a sound that emphasizes the 3rd and 5th harmonics, you are designing a bandpass filter centered at 3f₀ and 5f₀.

The spectral viewpoint makes it clear that subtractive synthesis — passing oscillator waveforms through filters — is a direct manipulation of harmonic content. The oscillator provides the harmonics; the filter chooses which ones to keep.

Practical Note: Analog vs. Digital Sawtooth

An analog sawtooth contains harmonics all the way up to the limits of the circuit, typically limited by the slew rate of the waveform and the parasitic capacitances in the oscillator. The harmonic content is physically continuous.

A digital sawtooth must be band-limited to avoid aliasing. Common techniques include:

  • BLIT (Band-Limited Impulse Train): Synthesize the discontinuities directly using sinc functions
  • MinBLEP (Minimum-Phase BLEP): Reduce the bandwidth of the discontinuity using precomputed correction tables
  • Wavetable synthesis: Store a single-cycle waveform at a sample rate where no aliasing can occur, then read it back at the desired pitch

Each approach affects the high-frequency character differently. The differences between analog and digital sawtooth sounds are real, and this is why.
