Sine Waves and Harmonic Content

Why the sine wave is fundamental and how all periodic sounds decompose into harmonics

The Simplest Periodic Motion

Press a key. The speaker cone moves back and forth, and that motion propagates through air as pressure variations. If the cone moves in exactly the shape described by a sine function — smoothly accelerating toward the center, decelerating at the extremes — the result is a pure tone: a single frequency with no other components. This is a sine wave.

Everything in acoustics and synthesis ultimately comes back to sine waves, for a reason that is mathematical rather than arbitrary.

Fourier’s Theorem

In 1822, Joseph Fourier proved that any periodic waveform can be expressed as a sum of sine waves — specifically, a sine wave at the fundamental frequency plus additional sine waves at integer multiples of that frequency. Those integer multiples are the harmonics (sometimes called partials or overtones).

If your fundamental is 100 Hz, the harmonic series is:

This is not a coincidence — the intervals of Western music are largely derived from the harmonic series because acoustic instruments produce this pattern of partials naturally.

Why Sine Waves Are Special

The sine wave is the only waveform that passes through a linear system (like a filter or an amplifier) without changing its shape. A filter can change the amplitude and phase of a sine wave, but it remains a sine wave. Any other waveform shape is really just a collection of sine waves at different frequencies, and a filter changes those frequencies independently.

This is why engineers and physicists care so much about sinusoidal analysis. When you understand how a system responds to sine waves at every frequency, you understand how it responds to everything.

Building Waveforms from Harmonics

The canonical synthesizer waveforms are distinguished entirely by which harmonics they contain and at what amplitudes:

Sine wave: Only the fundamental. No harmonics. The smoothest possible periodic waveform.

Square wave: Odd harmonics only (1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th…), each at amplitude 1/n where n is the harmonic number. The 3rd harmonic is 1/3 the amplitude of the fundamental, the 5th is 1/5, and so on.

Sawtooth wave: All harmonics (1st, 2nd, 3rd…) at amplitude 1/n. The most harmonically rich of the standard waveforms.

Triangle wave: Odd harmonics only like a square, but amplitude falls off as 1/n². Much softer-sounding than a square because higher harmonics are suppressed faster.

What This Means for Synthesis

When you run a sawtooth wave through a low-pass filter, you are selectively removing harmonics from the top of the series. The filter’s cutoff frequency determines where the series gets truncated; the resonance determines whether there is a boost at the cutoff. The character of the sound is determined by which harmonics survive.

When you change the pitch of an oscillator, the entire harmonic series shifts proportionally. If the fundamental doubles (an octave up), every harmonic doubles with it. The timbre — the relationship between harmonics — stays the same.

This is why a violin sounds like a violin across its entire range: same instrument, same physical structure producing the same harmonic ratios, just shifted up or down in frequency.

Spectrum vs. Waveform

The oscilloscope shows you a waveform in the time domain — voltage as a function of time. The spectrum analyzer shows you the same signal in the frequency domain — amplitude as a function of frequency. These are two views of identical information; one can be derived from the other using the Fourier transform.

A synthesist comfortable reading both views can understand the sound before hearing it. The waveform shows you the shape of each cycle; the spectrum shows you immediately which harmonics are present and how strong they are.

Alias and the Sampling Problem

Digital synthesizers produce waveforms by generating sample values at fixed intervals (the sample rate). This creates a hard upper frequency limit: the Nyquist frequency, equal to half the sample rate. A standard sample rate of 44.1 kHz can represent frequencies up to 22.05 kHz.

A naive digital sawtooth wave at 440 Hz contains harmonics at 880, 1320, 1760… continuing past 22 kHz. Harmonics that exceed the Nyquist frequency cannot be represented correctly; they fold back to lower frequencies, producing audible artifacts called aliasing. This is why digital oscillators use band-limited synthesis techniques — they omit harmonics that would alias.

An analog oscillator has no such limit, which is one reason why the harmonic content of analog and digital sawtooth waves sounds different, particularly in the upper octaves.
