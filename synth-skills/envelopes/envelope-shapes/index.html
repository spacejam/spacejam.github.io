Envelope Shape and Perception

How attack shape determines the perceived character of a transient, and why some instruments need more than four stages

Beyond ADSR

The four-stage ADSR envelope covers an enormous range of acoustic behavior, but it is a simplification. Real acoustic instruments have complex amplitude profiles that do not always fit neatly into attack/decay/sustain/release. Understanding where ADSR is sufficient and where it breaks down clarifies both its usefulness and its limitations.

Attack Character and Instrument Identity

The attack transient — the first few milliseconds of a sound — is disproportionately important for instrument recognition. Experiments in psychoacoustics have shown that listeners can often identify instruments by their attacks alone, even when the sustain and release are muted.

The attack is important because it carries information about the physical excitation mechanism:

  • Plucked strings (guitar, harp): Very fast attack, immediate decay onset. The string velocity is maximum at the moment of release from the finger or pick.
  • Bowed strings (violin, cello): Slower, curved attack as the bow overcomes static friction. The Helmholtz motion of the string builds gradually.
  • Blown instruments (flute, oboe): Variable attack depending on technique. Can be extremely fast (tongue articulation) or gradual (air pressure only).
  • Struck percussion (xylophone, piano): Fast attack, then a clean decay with no sustain.

The ADSR model captures these broadly: short attack + short decay for plucked; longer attack for bowed; short attack + zero sustain for struck. But the shape within each stage is fixed by the analog RC circuit, not freely variable.

The Sustain Level and Perceived Loudness

Sustain level has a significant effect on perceived dynamics that is easy to overlook. Consider two patches with identical Attack and Decay times but different Sustain levels:

  • High sustain (70%): The decay stage barely audible; the sound jumps to near-peak and holds there.
  • Low sustain (10%): The decay stage dominates; most of the note’s energy is in the initial transient.

The second patch sounds more percussive even though the Attack time is the same. This is because the proportion of the note’s energy in the transient is higher — the note “says its name” quickly and then mostly rests.

For a realistic piano patch, sustain is typically 0% — real piano notes do not have a steady-state phase. The envelope is essentially a slow exponential decay with no fixed sustain level. The ADSR model handles this by setting sustain low and making decay very long.

Multi-Stage Envelopes

Some synthesizers provide additional envelope stages for finer control. The Oberheim OB-X and its descendants offered an ADSR with variable curve shapes per stage. The Yamaha DX7 used a six-operator structure where each operator had a four-stage envelope with rate and level for each stage, giving eight total parameters.

A generalized multi-stage envelope can be described as a sequence of (time, target level) pairs. Common extensions:

AHDSR (Attack-Hold-Decay-Sustain-Release): Adds a hold stage after attack — the envelope stays at peak level for a fixed time before beginning decay. Useful for simulating instruments that have a brief stable state at maximum amplitude.

DADSR (Delay-Attack-Decay-Sustain-Release): Adds an initial delay before attack. Each operator in an FM patch can have a different delay, allowing harmonic components to appear at different times — the physical behavior of a piano, where higher harmonics appear and disappear faster than lower ones.

ADBDR (Attack-Decay₁-Break-Decay₂-Release): The Korg EG-type. Adds a “break point” level partway through decay, then continues to sustain at a different rate. Allows asymmetric decay shapes — fast decay to break point, slow to sustain — that are difficult to approximate with standard ADSR.

Loop Envelopes

Some envelope generators can loop — when the release stage completes, they restart from attack. A looping envelope is a complex LFO with a free-running shape. The resulting modulation waveform is asymmetric (attack time ≠ decay time) and can have a hold at the peak.

The Clavia Nord Lead was notable for its looping ADSR, which allowed rhythmic amplitude patterns synchronized to the note. Used at audio rates, a looping envelope becomes a waveshaper operating on the amplitude.

Amplitude vs. Timbre Envelopes

The most important insight for synthesis design is that envelopes can control any parameter, and using them on multiple parameters independently creates instrument character.

A realistic piano requires at minimum:

  1. Amplitude envelope: fast attack, long decay, zero sustain
  2. Filter envelope: fast attack, faster decay than amplitude — the high harmonics fade faster than the fundamental, just as they do on a real piano

A realistic brass instrument:

  1. Amplitude: moderate attack, full sustain, moderate release
  2. Filter: fast attack + fast decay with low sustain; or modulation to the filter that mirrors the performer’s lip pressure
  3. Pitch: slight initial sag then rise to pitch — the brass instrument’s “lipping” behavior

The more independent parameters can be enveloped, the closer the approximation to acoustic behavior. FM synthesis achieves its realism partly because each harmonic operator has an independent envelope, allowing the overtone structure to evolve in time independently — the way it does in acoustic instruments.
