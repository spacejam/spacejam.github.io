ADSR Mechanics

What RC circuits have to do with attack and decay, why exponential curves sound natural, and what the sustain stage really is

What an Envelope Does

When you press a key, the synthesizer needs to answer a question: how should the amplitude (and other parameters) change over time? A held piano note has a fast attack, a short decay, a sustained level, and a gradual release. A plucked string has a fast attack and a long, decaying release with no sustain stage at all. A bowed string has a slow attack and a flat, sustained level with an abrupt release.

The ADSR envelope generator encodes four stages of this time-varying shape:

  • Attack: Time to rise from 0 to peak
  • Decay: Time to fall from peak to sustain level
  • Sustain: Level held while the key is pressed (not a time)
  • Release: Time to fall from sustain level to 0 after key release

The Attack, Decay, and Release stages are time parameters. Sustain is a level parameter. This asymmetry is often misunderstood.

The RC Circuit Model

The simplest possible attack stage is a capacitor charging through a resistor toward a target voltage. The voltage across the capacitor follows:

V(t) = V_target × (1 − e^(−t/τ))

where τ = RC is the time constant of the circuit. After one time constant, the voltage reaches 63% of its target. After five time constants, it’s essentially at 99.3%.

This is an exponential approach to a target — not a linear ramp. The rate of change is fastest at the beginning and slows as the target is approached.

For decay, the capacitor discharges:

V(t) = V_start × e^(−t/τ)

Again exponential, but falling. Fastest at the beginning, slowing toward zero.

Many analog envelope generators are literally RC circuits with switched targets. Gate on: charge toward the attack peak. Peak reached: switch target to sustain level and let the capacitor discharge (decay stage). Gate off: switch target to zero (release stage).

Why Exponential Curves Sound Natural

The exponential shape is not arbitrary. It has a perceptual property that makes it sound natural: the relative rate of change is constant.

An exponential decay has the property that it loses the same fraction of its value in every equal time interval. If it loses half its value in the first 100ms, it loses half of the remainder in the next 100ms, and so on.

The ear perceives loudness logarithmically — doubling amplitude corresponds to +6 dB and sounds like a roughly equal step regardless of starting level. An exponential decay, perceived on a log scale, appears as a linear fade. This is why acoustic instruments (whose resonances decay exponentially by physics) sound smooth and natural, while a digitally generated linear ramp fade often sounds awkward.

Attack Shape: Linear vs. Exponential

The attack stage is a more complex perceptual case. A linear attack (rising at a constant rate) sounds slightly faster than an exponential attack of the same nominal attack time, because the linear ramp reaches high amplitudes sooner.

Some synthesizers offer both. A convex (logarithmic) attack curve is slow at the start and fast at the end — this is the shape of a bowed string entry, where friction takes time to overcome before the string begins vibrating strongly. A concave (exponential) curve rises fast initially then decelerates — more like a plucked string.

In digital envelope generators, the shape is a design choice. Analog RC circuits naturally produce the concave (exponential) curve.

The Sustain Is Not a Stage

Sustain is commonly drawn as the third segment of an ADSR diagram, but it is not a transition — it is a condition. While the key is held, the envelope holds at the sustain level indefinitely. No RC charging or discharging occurs. The envelope generator is simply outputting a fixed DC voltage.

This means the sustain level has no inherent “shape” to it. What happens during the sustain depends entirely on what other modulation is applied to the parameter the envelope is controlling.

Attack Time vs. Amplitude

There is a subtle interaction: the attack time parameter specifies how long the attack takes, but the envelope’s starting point matters. If the synthesizer receives a new note before the previous note has fully released, and the envelope is retriggered from its current value, the attack starts from a nonzero level — and reaches peak faster (in absolute time) than a fresh attack from zero.

This behavior varies between synthesizer designs. Some retrigger from zero always; some retrigger from the current level. The latter produces the legato or portamento effect where overlapping notes have a different character from cleanly separated notes.

Multiple Envelope Generators

Professional synthesizers typically provide multiple independent ADSR generators. A common configuration:

  • Envelope 1 → amplitude (VCA): controls whether you hear anything
  • Envelope 2 → filter cutoff (VCF): controls the spectral evolution
  • Envelope 3 → pitch: used for the sharp initial pitch sweep characteristic of bass drums and other percussive sounds

The filter envelope is often the most musically significant. A fast attack with a long decay creates the “wah” sweep heard in classic synthesizer bass lines — the filter opens and closes in synchrony with the note’s life cycle, mimicking the way an acoustic instrument’s harmonic structure changes from the initial transient to the sustain phase.

Envelope Scaling with Key

On many synthesizers, the envelope decay and release times scale with key position: notes higher on the keyboard have shorter decay times. This mimics the behavior of acoustic instruments, where higher-pitched strings are shorter and therefore ring for less time. On a piano, the high treble notes decay far more quickly than low bass notes, and the synthesizer’s key scaling approximates this by shortening the envelope for higher pitches.
