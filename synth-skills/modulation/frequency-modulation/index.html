FM Synthesis and Sidebands

The mathematics of FM synthesis: how modulation index determines sideband amplitudes via Bessel functions, and why operator ratios define timbre

FM Is Not Just Vibrato

Frequency modulation synthesis was invented by John Chowning at Stanford in 1967 and licensed to Yamaha, where it became the engine behind the DX7, one of the most influential synthesizers ever made. The key insight is that FM at audio rates produces a rich harmonic spectrum from a pair of sine waves — a spectrum that would require many sine waves to produce by additive synthesis.

At slow modulation rates (vibrato), FM produces only a barely-audible pitch variation. At audio rates, it creates sidebands. At high modulation depth, it creates many sidebands at precise, predictable frequencies. This is the basis of FM synthesis.

The FM Equation

When a carrier oscillator at frequency f_c is frequency-modulated by a modulator oscillator at frequency f_m with peak deviation Δf:

x(t) = A × sin(2π f_c t + β × sin(2π f_m t))

where β = Δf / f_m is the modulation index.

This looks simple but produces a complex spectrum. Expanding the outer sine function using the Jacobi-Anger expansion gives:

x(t) = A × Σ Jₙ(β) × sin(2π (f_c + n×f_m) t)
       n = −∞ to +∞

where Jₙ(β) is the Bessel function of the first kind of order n, evaluated at β.

In plain terms: the output is the sum of sinusoids at frequencies f_c + n×f_m for all integers n, with amplitudes determined by Bessel functions.

Sideband Frequencies

The sidebands appear at:

f_c, f_c ± f_m, f_c ± 2f_m, f_c ± 3f_m, ...

For a carrier at 440 Hz and modulator at 110 Hz:

  • f_c = 440 Hz
  • Sidebands at 330, 220, 110, 0… (below) and 550, 660, 770… (above)

The negative frequencies (0 Hz and below) fold back to positive frequencies and may add to or subtract from existing sidebands, depending on phase. This folding is part of what makes FM spectra complex and asymmetric.

Bessel Function Amplitudes

The Bessel functions J₀, J₁, J₂… determine how energy is distributed among the carrier and sidebands:

β = 0 (no modulation): J₀(0) = 1, all other Jₙ(0) = 0. Output is pure carrier.

β = 0.5 (light modulation): Most energy in carrier, first sidebands at ~25% of carrier amplitude, second sidebands negligible.

β = 1.5 (moderate modulation): Carrier and first two sidebands have comparable amplitude.

β = 2.4 (first carrier null): J₀(2.4) ≈ 0 — the carrier frequency disappears! All energy is in sidebands. The DX7’s electric piano patch uses modulation indices near this value for certain stages.

β = 5 (deep modulation): Many sidebands with appreciable amplitude, extending far above and below the carrier.

The total energy in the signal is conserved regardless of β — redistributed among sidebands, never created or destroyed.

Operator Ratios and Timbre

The ratio f_m / f_c determines whether the sidebands fall on harmonics of the carrier or at inharmonic (non-integer-multiple) positions.

Integer ratio (e.g., f_m = 2 × f_c): Sidebands land on harmonics of the carrier’s fundamental. The spectrum is harmonic — the sound has a definite pitch and is musically useful for most sounds.

Simple non-integer ratio (e.g., f_m / f_c = 1.414 ≈ √2): Sidebands land between harmonics. The spectrum is inharmonic — the sound has no clear fundamental pitch and tends to sound metallic or bell-like. The Yamaha DX7’s bell patches use ratios like 3.5:1 or 5:1 with the modulator at a non-harmonic ratio.

Ratio of 1:1 (modulator = carrier frequency): Every sideband falls on a harmonic or at DC. The timbre is harmonic and the sound can range from clarinet-like (low β) to very bright (high β).

Ratio of 1:7 (modulator = 7× carrier): Sidebands at 7, 14, 21… harmonic positions — meaning most harmonics are absent. The spectrum has a characteristic hollow, comb-like quality.

The C:M Notation

FM synthesis conventionally describes algorithms using the carrier:modulator ratio notation. A 1:1 ratio means the modulator and carrier are at the same frequency. A 1:2 ratio means the modulator is twice the carrier frequency.

Yamaha DX-series synthesizers use “algorithms” — specific routing configurations of their six operator stages. Operators can be carriers, modulators, or both. Common configurations:

  • Stack (A → B → C → D): Each operator modulates the next. Maximum harmonic complexity from the final modulator cascading through all stages.
  • Parallel (A+B+C → D): Multiple modulators independently modulate a single carrier. Each adds its own sidebands.
  • Feedback (A → A): Operator modulates itself. A pure sine at zero feedback becomes a progressively more sawtooth-like waveform as feedback increases. Maximum feedback produces white noise.

Envelopes in FM

Each operator in an FM synth has an independent amplitude envelope. This is the key to FM’s realism: as the modulator’s amplitude decreases over time, its modulation index decreases, and the sideband count decreases. The spectrum changes from bright and complex at the start to simpler and purer as the note decays.

This is exactly what happens in acoustic instruments: the attack transient has many inharmonic partials that fade quickly, leaving the fundamental and lower harmonics to sustain. A piano’s high notes have an attack envelope that decays to near-silence within a few seconds; the FM model replicates this with an envelope on the modulator operators.

FM vs. Physical Reality

FM synthesis is abstract — there is no direct analog in acoustic instrument physics. No acoustic instrument is literally a frequency-modulated oscillator. The reason FM works is that the sideband spectra it produces happen to resemble spectra found in real instruments, not because the model is physically accurate.

This is both strength and limitation. The strength: FM produces spectra that are difficult to create otherwise, and the parameter space is compact (six operators × eight envelope parameters per operator = 48 knobs controlling a highly complex timbral space). The limitation: because the model is not physically grounded, it is hard to design patches intuitively — the relationship between parameters and perceived result is not obvious without mathematical understanding or extensive experimentation.
