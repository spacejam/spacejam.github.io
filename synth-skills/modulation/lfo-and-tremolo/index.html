LFOs, Tremolo, and Vibrato

Low-frequency oscillators and the mathematics of amplitude and frequency modulation below the audio range

Oscillators Below Pitch

An oscillator running at 440 Hz produces a pitch — A above middle C. Run the same oscillator at 5 Hz and you hear not a pitch but a rate of change: five cycles per second, slow enough that you can follow individual cycles, too fast to be rhythmically meaningful.

This is the LFO (Low Frequency Oscillator): an oscillator whose purpose is not to produce audio but to periodically vary some other parameter. LFO rates typically span 0.01 Hz (one cycle per hundred seconds) to around 20 Hz (just below the audio threshold), though some synthesizers allow LFOs into audio rate.

Tremolo: Amplitude Modulation

Tremolo is the periodic variation of amplitude. The LFO controls the VCA:

amplitude(t) = A_base × (1 + depth × sin(2π × f_lfo × t))

At depth = 0, the amplitude is constant. At depth = 1, the amplitude varies from 0 to 2×A_base. In practice, the depth is limited so the amplitude never reaches zero (depth < 1), producing a pulse-like effect at full depth.

Tremolo is strictly an amplitude effect. It does not change the frequency content of the signal, only its loudness over time.

Tremolo vs. Amplitude Modulation in Audio

When the LFO rate increases above roughly 20 Hz, tremolo becomes ring modulation — a different effect altogether. At audio rates, multiplying two signals together creates sidebands: new frequencies at (f_carrier ± f_lfo). These sidebands are audible as separate tones, not as a rate of amplitude change.

The transition from tremolo to ring modulation is not abrupt — there is a perceptual threshold region around 15–25 Hz where the effect changes character. This is also the range where rhythmic patterns transition to continuous tones (low bass), which is why bass guitars and synthesizer bass patches at very low pitches sometimes sound more rhythmic than pitched.

Vibrato: Frequency Modulation

Vibrato is the periodic variation of pitch. The LFO controls the oscillator frequency or pitch CV:

frequency(t) = f_base × 2^(depth × sin(2π × f_lfo × t) / 12)

The ÷12 converts semitones to octaves — depth in this formula is the modulation depth in semitones. A depth of 1 semitone means the pitch varies ±1 semitone (a whole tone range) around its center.

Note that the formula uses a multiplicative relationship with the base frequency: the deviation is proportional to the pitch. An LFO that produces ±50 cents at A3 (440 Hz) produces ±50 cents at A4 (880 Hz) as well. The modulation tracks the pitch, maintaining constant musical deviation.

This is in contrast to additive frequency modulation, where a fixed Hz deviation would produce a large interval at low pitches and a tiny interval at high pitches.

Vibrato vs. Frequency Modulation at Audio Rate

Like tremolo and ring modulation, vibrato and FM synthesis are the same mathematical operation at different rates.

At slow LFO rates, the ear follows the pitch variation as vibrato. As the rate increases past ~20 Hz, the ear cannot track individual cycles and instead hears the sidebands that FM creates:

sidebands at: f_carrier ± n × f_modulator (for n = 1, 2, 3...)

The amplitudes of these sidebands are given by Bessel functions of the first kind. This is why Yamaha FM synthesis produces its characteristic spectrum — the modulation index (equivalent to depth) determines the relative amplitudes of the sideband pairs.

A vibrato depth of 0.5 semitones at 5 Hz is musically expressive. The same depth at 100 Hz produces a cluster of sidebands at 440 ± 100n Hz with amplitudes J₀(small), J₁(small), J₂(very small)… — barely audible. But at high modulation index (deep FM), the sidebands extend far and are loud.

LFO Shape and Modulation Character

Different LFO waveforms produce different modulation characters:

Sine: Smooth, symmetrical variation. Sounds organic and is the most common choice for vibrato and tremolo.

Triangle: Linear ramp between extremes. Slightly harder-edged than sine but still smooth.

Square: Abrupt switching between two levels. Used for “keyboard click” effects, helicopter-like tremolo, or pitch trills.

Sawtooth: Gradually sweeps in one direction then resets. Creates a rhythmic, directional sweep — useful for filter modulation where you want a rising sweep that suddenly resets.

Random (sample-and-hold): Holds a random value for one LFO cycle, then jumps to a new random value. Produces the stepping random pitch effect heard in “sci-fi computer” sounds and classic arpeggio-like randomness.

Phase and Multiple Oscillators

When multiple oscillators are all vibrato-modulated by the same LFO, they all move in pitch together — the modulation is coherent. This sounds like a single pitch-varying entity.

If the LFO is phase-shifted for each oscillator (LFO 1 at 0°, LFO 2 at 120°, LFO 3 at 240°), the oscillators sweep in a rotating relationship. This is the chorus effect: multiple detuned oscillators with phase-staggered LFOs produce a lush, wide sound because at any moment the pitch relationships are constantly changing.

A physical string section produces exactly this effect naturally: each violinist has independent involuntary vibrato timing, and the result is the characteristic shimmer of a string ensemble that no single violin can produce.

Tempo Synchronization

When the LFO is synchronized to the tempo of a sequencer, the modulation becomes rhythmically meaningful. An LFO at 1 cycle per beat produces one tremolo pulsation per beat. At 1/4 cycle per beat, the modulation completes a full swing over four beats — a slow filter sweep tied to musical phrase timing.

Most modern synthesizers offer LFO sync options in terms of musical time values (1/4 note, 1/8 note, dotted 1/4, triplet 1/8, etc.) rather than absolute Hz rates. The advantage is that changing the tempo doesn’t break the musical relationship between the modulation and the rhythm.
