The State-Variable Filter

How a single filter topology simultaneously produces lowpass, highpass, bandpass, and notch outputs — and why

Phase visualization

Four Outputs for the Price of One

Most filter designs commit to a single output type. The state-variable filter (SVF) is different: it simultaneously produces four responses — lowpass, highpass, bandpass, and notch (band-reject) — from the same input signal, all at the same cutoff frequency and resonance.

This is not an accident. The four outputs are relationships that must exist together, as a consequence of the mathematics.

The Topology

The state-variable filter consists of two integrators in a feedback loop with a summing amplifier. In continuous-time form, the three state variables evolve as:

HP = input - (Q⁻¹ × BP) - LP
BP = ∫ HP
LP = ∫ BP

Where HP, BP, and LP are the highpass, bandpass, and lowpass outputs respectively, and Q is the quality factor (inverse of damping).

The notch output is simply:

Notch = HP + LP

Because the highpass and lowpass outputs add constructively everywhere except near the cutoff frequency (where they cancel), the notch is a natural consequence.

Why Four Simultaneous Outputs?

Consider what happens to a sine wave at three different frequencies:

  • Well below cutoff: The input passes through both integrators, which at low frequencies act as passive elements. LP is large, BP and HP are small.
  • At cutoff: Energy circulates in the loop. BP is at maximum, LP and HP are equal and 90° apart from BP.
  • Well above cutoff: The integrators attenuate heavily at high frequencies. HP is large, LP and BP are small.

These relationships are constraints — not design choices. If LP is defined by double integration of the input, and HP is defined as “everything that didn’t go through,” and BP is defined as single integration of HP, then the four responses are determined simultaneously. There is no way to have one without the others.

Phase Relationships

Each output is a phase-shifted version of the input, and the phases differ:

OutputPhase at cutoff
Highpass0°
Bandpass−90°
Lowpass−180°
Notchvaries (0° below cutoff, −180° above)

The bandpass output is always exactly 90° behind the highpass and 90° ahead of the lowpass. This is the mathematical signature of a resonant system — energy sloshing back and forth between two storage elements (the two integrators), exactly like a mass-spring system.

The Quality Factor

The Q parameter controls resonance. In filter terms:

Q = f_cutoff / bandwidth

where bandwidth is the −3 dB width of the bandpass peak. A Q of 1 gives a bandwidth of one octave centered at the cutoff. A Q of 10 gives a narrow peak one-tenth of an octave wide.

As Q increases, the bandpass peak sharpens and the lowpass rolloff before the cutoff becomes more pronounced (the characteristic resonant hump). At Q = 0.707 (or 1/√2), the lowpass response is maximally flat — this is the Butterworth condition.

At Q → ∞, the filter self-oscillates at the cutoff frequency, exactly as with the ladder filter. The difference is that the SVF self-oscillates cleanly across its entire frequency range — the ladder filter’s self-oscillation varies in character depending on frequency because the four poles are not perfectly independent.

Cutoff Frequency Control

The cutoff frequency of an SVF is controlled by a single parameter that simultaneously scales the integrators. This gives exact, linear frequency control. Doubling the control parameter exactly halves the cutoff frequency.

In analog SVF designs, this parameter is typically a transconductance (gm) — a current-to-voltage conversion ratio controlled by a CV input. Many state-variable synthesizer filters use OTAs (Operational Transconductance Amplifiers) for this purpose.

In digital implementations, the integrators become delay elements, and the frequency parameter scales the delay length. The Chamberlin state-variable filter (a discrete-time version) was widely used in classic synthesizer chips for exactly this reason — precise, stable frequency control with a single coefficient.

Comparing SVF and Ladder

Both filters can achieve resonance and self-oscillation. The differences are in character:

Ladder filter: Each pole is at the same frequency, creating a −24 dB/octave lowpass with a characteristic “trumpet-like” resonance. The resonance sound is complex because the output is taken from after all four stages, and the internal voltages are highly nonlinear at high resonance.

State-variable filter: The resonance is cleaner and more precise. The four outputs offer flexibility the ladder does not. Many digital synthesizers use SVF variants because their behavior is mathematically clean and their cutoff control is exact.

Some synthesizers (notably the Oberheim SEM) mix outputs of the SVF continuously — blending between lowpass, notch, and highpass — giving a continuously variable filter character across a single knob sweep.

The Notch as a Comb

At very high Q, the bandpass output becomes very narrow. The notch, being HP + LP, develops a very sharp null. If the cutoff frequency is swept rhythmically, the notch produces a phaser-like effect — the sharp dip moves through the spectrum.

At even higher Q (or with multiple SVF stages in series), the notch can be made narrow enough to nearly eliminate a single frequency while leaving everything else intact. This is useful for feedback removal and is why parametric equalizers are built on SVF or related topologies.
