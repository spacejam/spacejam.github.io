Filter Poles, Zeros, and Slopes

What filter order really means, where poles and zeros come from, and how to read a Bode plot

Phase visualization

The −6 dB/Octave Rule

A single-pole lowpass filter attenuates at −6 dB per octave above its cutoff frequency. Add another pole and you get −12 dB/octave. Four poles: −24 dB/octave. The rule is exact: each pole contributes −6 dB/octave, and the contributions add.

But why 6 dB per octave, and what is a pole?

RC Filters and Poles

The simplest possible filter is a resistor and capacitor in series, with the output taken across the capacitor. Its transfer function — the ratio of output to input voltage as a function of frequency — is:

H(s) = 1 / (1 + sRC)

where s = jω = j × 2πf is the complex frequency variable. The pole of this transfer function is the value of s that makes the denominator zero:

s_pole = −1/RC

In the frequency domain (s = jω), the magnitude response is:

|H(jω)| = 1 / √(1 + (ωRC)²)

At ω = 1/RC (the cutoff frequency), the denominator is √2, so the output is 1/√2 of the input — that’s −3 dB. Above this frequency, for each doubling of ω, the denominator roughly doubles, so the output halves — which is −6 dB per octave.

What a Pole Does Geometrically

In the complex s-plane, a pole at s = −a means: as frequency increases (moving up the imaginary axis), the distance from the operating point to the pole increases. That increasing distance causes the response to fall.

A pole on the negative real axis (like the RC filter) causes a gradual, smooth rolloff. A pole with an imaginary component (a complex pole) causes a resonant bump before rolloff — the operating frequency passes close to the pole before moving away, and nearby poles amplify the response.

The ladder filter’s resonance comes from moving its poles toward the imaginary axis by feeding back energy. At maximum resonance, the poles hit the imaginary axis and the filter oscillates — poles on the imaginary axis mean undamped oscillation.

Zeros: The Opposite of Poles

A zero is a frequency where the transfer function equals zero — the filter completely rejects that frequency. A notch filter has zeros at its null frequency. A highpass filter has a zero at DC (ω = 0).

The phase behavior of a filter depends on the locations of both poles and zeros:

  • A pole near the operating frequency causes the phase to decrease (lag).
  • A zero causes the phase to increase (lead) — or cause a rapid transition through 180°.

Filters with no zeros (all-pole filters) are called minimum-phase filters. Their phase response is completely determined by their magnitude response — you cannot independently control the two. The ladder filter and state-variable filter are minimum-phase.

All-pass filters have equal numbers of poles and zeros, carefully arranged so that magnitudes cancel but phases do not. They pass all frequencies at equal amplitude but impose frequency-dependent delays. Phaser effects are all-pass filter stages in a feedback loop.

Reading a Bode Plot

A Bode plot shows magnitude (in dB) and phase (in degrees) as functions of log frequency. The conventions:

Magnitude plot:

  • Flat region = frequencies the filter passes
  • Rolloff = frequencies the filter attenuates
  • Slope = −20 dB/decade per pole (which is the same as −6 dB/octave, since one decade ≈ 3.32 octaves and −20/3.32 ≈ −6)
  • A resonant peak shows up as a bump before the rolloff

Phase plot:

  • Each pole contributes up to −90° of phase shift
  • A resonant pair of poles (complex poles) causes a rapid phase transition near the cutoff
  • At very high frequency, a 4-pole lowpass filter has −360° of phase shift (four poles × −90°)

The phase shift matters for synthesis because it determines how the filter’s output relates in time to its input. At high resonance, the bandpass output of an SVF is 90° behind the input at cutoff — if you route this output back to the input, you change the effective feedback gain of the patch in ways that depend on frequency.

Higher-order filters have sharper transitions but more phase shift. The −360° of phase from a 4-pole filter means the output can be 180° out of phase with the input at certain frequencies — relevant when using filter resonance as a feedback element.

The Cutoff Isn’t Where You Think

The −3 dB cutoff (the pole frequency) is where the filter response drops to 0.707 of its passband value. But the “sound” of the filter — the frequency where it noticeably changes the character of the signal — often appears to be higher, especially for low-Q filters.

This is because the ear is logarithmic in frequency (it hears equal pitch intervals as equal, not equal frequency differences) and roughly logarithmic in amplitude (it hears decibels, not linear amplitude ratios). The −1 dB point, which is well above the stated cutoff, is closer to where most listeners perceive the rolloff beginning.

Resonance shifts this perception dramatically. A high-Q filter sounds like it has a higher apparent cutoff because the resonant peak makes lower frequencies prominent — the contrast between the peak and the rolloff beyond it creates a sharper subjective boundary than the raw filter mathematics would suggest.
