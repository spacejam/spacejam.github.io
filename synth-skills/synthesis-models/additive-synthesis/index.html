Additive Synthesis

Building timbres from sine waves: how additive synthesis works, why it's flexible and expensive, and what it shares with the physics of acoustic instruments

The Direct Approach

If every periodic waveform is a sum of sine waves (Fourier’s theorem), then the most direct approach to synthesis is to explicitly specify which sine waves to sum and at what amplitudes. This is additive synthesis.

In principle, it is the most general possible synthesis method. Given enough oscillators and control, you can produce any sound. In practice, the challenge is the number of oscillators needed and the complexity of controlling them all meaningfully.

The Organ as Additive Synthesizer

The pipe organ is the oldest additive synthesizer. Each rank of pipes corresponds to a harmonic or subharmonic of the fundamental; the organist’s drawbars (on electronic organs like the Hammond B3) control how much of each rank is mixed in.

The Hammond’s nine drawbars correspond to:

Pulling a drawbar out increases the amplitude of that harmonic. Pushing it in removes it. The Hammond generates nine sine-like tones (tonewheels) at the harmonics; the player combines them in real time.

This is additive synthesis as an intuitive, real-time instrument. The drawbars are directly meaningful to a musician: “more upper harmonics = brighter sound.”

Time-Varying Additive Synthesis

A static additive synthesizer — harmonics at fixed amplitudes — produces a static timbre. The sound doesn’t evolve. Real acoustic instruments are interesting partly because their harmonic content changes over time: the attack has different harmonic content than the sustain; the high harmonics decay faster than the fundamental.

Time-varying additive synthesis assigns an independent amplitude envelope to each partial. The nth harmonic at frequency n×f₀ has amplitude Aₙ(t) that changes over time according to its own envelope.

A realistic piano model might use 100+ partials, each with a different decay rate. High partials decay in milliseconds; the fundamental may take seconds or minutes. This is computationally expensive but produces highly realistic results.

The Spectral Envelope

Rather than specifying each harmonic’s amplitude independently, many additive synthesizers use a spectral envelope — a smooth curve in the frequency domain that determines the amplitude of each harmonic based on its proximity to the curve.

This is exactly what the formants of a vocal tract are: a spectral envelope applied to the harmonic series of the vocal cords. The harmonics of the voice are the source; the resonant frequencies of the vocal tract (formants) are a spectral envelope that shapes which harmonics are loud.

A synthesizer with spectral envelope control can imitate vocal vowels by setting the formant frequencies to match those of different vowels:

  • Vowel /a/ (as in “father”): F1 ≈ 700 Hz, F2 ≈ 1200 Hz
  • Vowel /i/ (as in “see”): F1 ≈ 280 Hz, F2 ≈ 2500 Hz
  • Vowel /u/ (as in “boot”): F1 ≈ 320 Hz, F2 ≈ 800 Hz

The spectral envelope approach reduces many individual harmonic controls to a few formant frequencies and bandwidths — a much more compact and physically meaningful representation.

Phase and Perception

In static additive synthesis, the phases of the individual sine waves matter very little perceptually. Two waveforms with the same harmonic amplitudes but different phases sound identical to most listeners under most conditions.

This is a psychoacoustic fact (Ohm’s acoustic law, approximately): the ear is relatively insensitive to phase for steady-state sounds. However:

  1. During transients, phase relationships matter significantly. The attack of a sound has rapid changes, and phase misalignment can cause the initial waveform shape to look — and sound — different.

  2. In headphone listening, phase differences between left and right channels can create spatial effects unrelated to the intended timbre.

  3. At very low frequencies (below ~300 Hz), phase may become more audible because the ear may be following individual pressure waves.

For practical synthesis, starting all partials at zero phase is a common default and works well for most purposes.

Why Additive Synthesis Is Expensive

With N harmonics, you need N oscillators. Each oscillator needs:

  • A frequency (N×f₀, trivial)
  • An amplitude (set by envelope)
  • An envelope generator (the expensive part)

For a realistic instrument with 50–100 partials and complex amplitude envelopes, the computation is substantial. In the 1970s and 1980s, this was prohibitively expensive in hardware.

The first commercially successful additive synthesizer was the Fairlight CMI, which used resynthesis from recorded samples rather than envelope-controlled oscillators. The modern equivalent is the additive engine of software synthesizers like Camel Audio’s Alchemy or Native Instruments’ Absynth, where CPU cost is modest.

Connection to Subtractive Synthesis

Additive synthesis (specify which harmonics to include) and subtractive synthesis (start with all harmonics, remove the ones you don’t want) are complementary approaches. In practice:

  • Additive synthesis offers precise spectral control but requires many parameters.
  • Subtractive synthesis is simpler to control (one filter changes many harmonics at once) but is less flexible.

Many synthesizers combine both approaches: additive oscillators whose output feeds a subtractive filter. The oscillator mix (which waveforms, at what amplitudes) provides coarse additive shaping; the filter provides smooth, continuous subtractive control.
