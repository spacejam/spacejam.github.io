Physical Modeling and Karplus-Strong

How waveguide synthesis models the physics of vibrating strings and resonant tubes, and what Karplus-Strong actually computes

What Physical Modeling Is

Most synthesis approaches are abstract: FM synthesis uses mathematical relationships that happen to produce instrument-like spectra. Additive synthesis assembles sounds from sine waves. Neither model has a direct connection to acoustic physics.

Physical modeling takes a different approach: build a computational model of the physical system that produces sound. A plucked string model computes what a plucked string does — how the wave travels along the string, how it reflects from the ends, how it dissipates energy — using the same differential equations that govern the real string.

The result is not just a close imitation; it is, to the extent the model is accurate, the sound the system would produce. Physical modeling synthesis can produce articulations and timbral variations that are structurally impossible in FM or subtractive synthesis, because those variations emerge naturally from the physics.

The Waveguide Model

The fundamental tool for physical modeling of strings and tubes is the waveguide. A one-dimensional waveguide models wave propagation in one dimension: waves travel left and right along the structure, are delayed by the propagation time, and are reflected with some coefficient at the ends.

For a lossless ideal string of length L with wave speed c, the round-trip propagation time is:

T = 2L/c

This sets the fundamental frequency:

f₀ = 1/T = c/(2L)

The wave speed c depends on string tension and mass per unit length:

c = √(tension / linear mass density)

Tighter tension or lighter string → higher wave speed → higher pitch. This is why tightening a guitar string raises its pitch.

The Karplus-Strong Algorithm

In 1983, Kevin Karplus and Alex Strong published a startlingly simple algorithm for plucked string synthesis:

  1. Fill a short delay line (ring buffer) with random noise
  2. Each output sample is the average of consecutive samples in the buffer
  3. The buffer output is also the next buffer input (feedback loop)

The delay line length determines the period (and therefore the pitch). The averaging operation is a simple one-pole lowpass filter: it progressively removes high-frequency content from the loop. Over time, the high harmonics decay away and only the fundamental remains — exactly what happens on a real plucked string, where higher harmonics decay faster than lower ones.

In pseudo-code:

buffer = random_noise(length = sample_rate / frequency)
loop:
    output = buffer[read_head]
    buffer[write_head] = 0.5 × (buffer[read_head] + buffer[(read_head - 1) mod length])
    advance read_head and write_head

Despite its simplicity, this produces a convincingly plucked string sound.

Why It Works: Wave Decomposition

The noise burst in the delay line can be understood as an infinite sum of sinusoids at the harmonics of the fundamental (by Fourier theorem). The feedback loop reinforces these frequencies — specifically, frequencies whose wavelength divides evenly into the loop length — while the averaging filter attenuates each harmonic by a factor that increases with frequency.

At each pass through the loop, each harmonic is multiplied by a factor slightly less than 1 (its loop gain). The loop gain for the nth harmonic is:

g_n = |H(e^(j2πn/N))|

where H is the transfer function of the averaging filter and N is the loop length. Higher harmonics have lower loop gain and decay faster. This differential decay is what gives the plucked sound its characteristic timbre evolution — from bright-and-noisy at onset to pure-and-sinusoidal at long times.

Extensions: Bowed Strings

A bowed string model extends the plucked model with a nonlinear element at the bow contact point. The bow exerts a stick-slip force on the string: when string velocity matches bow velocity, the string sticks and moves with the bow; when the force exceeds static friction, the string slips back.

This stick-slip behavior is modeled by a nonlinear function at one point in the waveguide loop. The result is Helmholtz motion — the characteristic sawtooth-like string vibration that distinguishes bowed from plucked strings.

Bowed string models require feedback from string velocity to the bow force computation, making them more complex but also capable of modeling articulation: pressure, bow speed, and bow position all become meaningful control parameters that change the simulated physics.

Resonant Tubes: Wind Instruments

Wind instrument modeling uses the same waveguide structure but with different reflections. A tube open at both ends has antinodes (pressure minima) at both ends; closed at both ends has nodes (pressure maxima). A tube closed at one end (like a stopped organ pipe or a clarinet) has the special property that its resonance frequencies are at odd harmonics only — the same as a square wave.

The reed or lip is modeled as a nonlinear valve that connects the player’s breath pressure to the tube pressure. The reed opens when the pressure differential exceeds a threshold, admits a burst of air, and closes again. This burst excites the resonances of the tube.

Physical models of woodwinds can capture formant-like resonances of the bell (the flared end), finger hole effects, and the transition between different playing modes (register holes, overblowing) — behaviors that are structurally unavailable in purely spectral synthesis methods.

Modal Synthesis

For percussion instruments (bells, bars, plates), the vibration modes are not evenly spaced harmonics — they are at inharmonic frequencies determined by the geometry and material properties.

A bell vibrating mode at frequency fₙ decays exponentially with time constant τₙ. Modal synthesis represents the instrument as a sum of these decaying sinusoids:

x(t) = Σ Aₙ × e^(-t/τₙ) × sin(2π fₙ t)

Each mode has its own frequency, amplitude, and decay rate. For a realistic bell, the mode frequencies are experimentally measured or computed from finite-element analysis.

The synthesis is additive, but the mode frequencies are not integer multiples of a fundamental. The inharmonicity is what gives bells, marimbas, and other tuned percussion their characteristic timbre — recognizable as a specific pitch but with a distinctive “clang” quality that pure harmonic instruments lack.

Physical modeling research has produced highly accurate models of these instruments, to the point where synthesized and recorded bells can be difficult to distinguish on first listening.
