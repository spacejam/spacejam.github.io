The Harmonic Series

Why the overtone series is universal in acoustic instruments, how it defines musical intervals, and what happens when an instrument is inharmonic

Why Harmonics Are Everywhere

A vibrating string, an air column in a tube, a membrane — these physical systems all share a property: they support standing waves only at specific frequencies. A string of length L supports standing waves at frequencies where the wavelength is 2L/n for integer n. In terms of frequency:

fₙ = n × f₁   (n = 1, 2, 3, ...)

The frequencies are integer multiples of the lowest (fundamental) frequency. This is the harmonic series.

Why integer multiples? The boundary conditions force it. A string pinned at both ends has zero displacement at the ends, which means only waves whose half-wavelength divides evenly into L can form standing patterns. Any other frequency produces a traveling wave that destructively interferes with itself after one round trip.

The same mathematics applies to air columns in tubes, though the boundary conditions differ (pressure node vs. antinode at open vs. closed ends), and to the transverse modes of membranes and plates (with the additional complication that these have two-dimensional boundary conditions).

The Harmonic Series and Musical Intervals

The first few harmonics of any fundamental form intervals that appear in almost every musical tradition:

PairRatioInterval
1:22/1Octave
2:33/2Perfect fifth
3:44/3Perfect fourth
4:55/4Major third
5:66/5Minor third
4:77/4Harmonic seventh (near minor seventh)
8:99/8Major second

These are just intervals — intervals defined by simple frequency ratios. Pythagorean tuning uses only 3:2 ratios stacked on top of each other. Just intonation uses ratios derived from the first few harmonics. Equal temperament is a compromise that makes all intervals slightly impure to allow all keys to be equivalent.

The reason these intervals feel consonant is not purely cultural, though culture plays a role. When two tones are played together, their harmonics interact. Two notes a perfect fifth apart (3:2) have a harmonic series where every other harmonic of the upper note falls on a harmonic of the lower note — they mesh. Two notes a tritone apart share almost no harmonics, and where they come close, they are slightly misaligned, producing beating.

String Behavior: Stiffness and Inharmonicity

A perfectly flexible string vibrates at exactly integer multiples of its fundamental. Real strings are not perfectly flexible — they have stiffness (resistance to bending). Stiff strings resist the sharp bends that would be needed for pure standing waves, so higher modes vibrate at slightly higher frequencies than the harmonic prediction.

The deviation is called inharmonicity. For the nth mode:

fₙ = n × f₁ × √(1 + B × n²)

where B is the inharmonicity coefficient that depends on string diameter, material, and length. The higher the mode, the more its frequency deviates upward from the harmonic series.

This has important practical consequences:

  • Piano tuning is adjusted (piano tuners “stretch” the octaves) to account for inharmonicity. A high C on the piano is not exactly twice the frequency of the middle C on the same instrument; it needs to be tuned slightly sharper for the two to sound “in tune.”
  • Short, thick bass strings on upright pianos are significantly more inharmonic than the long, thin strings on concert grands. This is one reason large concert grand pianos sound different from upright pianos — the longer strings have less inharmonicity, so their overtones are more precisely harmonic.

Air Columns: Open and Closed Tubes

A tube open at both ends (like a flute or an organ flue pipe) has pressure antinodes at both ends. Its resonance frequencies are:

fₙ = n × c/(2L)   (n = 1, 2, 3, ...)

All harmonics — same as a string.

A tube closed at one end (like a clarinet or a stopped organ pipe) has a pressure node at the closed end and antinode at the open end. Only odd harmonics survive:

fₙ = n × c/(4L)   (n = 1, 3, 5, ...)

The closed tube also has an effective length of 4L rather than 2L, so for the same physical length, the closed tube’s fundamental is an octave lower.

This is why clarinets overblown at the octave do not overblow to the octave (2×f₁) but to the twelfth (3×f₁, the next available odd harmonic). Saxophones, with conical bores, do overblow to the octave because the conical geometry restores the full harmonic series.

When the Harmonic Series Fails: Bells and Plates

For strings and tubes, the boundary conditions produce exact integer-multiple harmonics. For two-dimensional systems (circular membranes, plates, bells), the boundary conditions are more complex and the resonance frequencies are not integer multiples.

For a circular membrane, the mode frequencies are given by zeros of Bessel functions:

f_mn ∝ z_mn / r

where z_mn are the Bessel function zeros (2.405, 3.832, 5.136…) rather than integers (1, 2, 3…). These are inharmonic — not related to the fundamental by simple ratios.

This is why drums don’t have a clear pitch (the membrane modes are inharmonic) while metallophones like the glockenspiel or vibraphone have been physically engineered to have harmonically related modes. On the vibraphone, the cross-sections are carefully calculated to tune the first overtone to an octave above the fundamental — what would naturally be inharmonic has been forced into harmonic alignment by shaping the bar.

Bells are similarly engineered. A church bell is cast and then tuned by removing metal at specific points until its lowest five modes (called hum, prime, tierce, quint, and nominal) fall into a specific, traditional inharmonic relationship that is perceived as consonant.

Implications for Synthesis

Understanding the harmonic series helps explain why certain synthesis approaches work:

  • Subtractive synthesis starts with all harmonics (sawtooth) and removes them; it works because the source already has the harmonic structure of a periodic waveform.
  • FM synthesis can produce harmonic or inharmonic spectra depending on the operator ratio — integer ratios are harmonic, non-integer ratios are not.
  • Physical modeling automatically produces inharmonicity when the model includes string stiffness, making it more realistic than mathematical approaches that assume ideal harmonics.
  • Additive synthesis of a bell requires knowing its inharmonic mode frequencies, which must be measured or computed — they cannot be derived from a simple formula.
