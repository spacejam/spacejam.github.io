Resonance, Formants, and the Vocal Tract

How resonant cavities shape spectra, what vocal formants are and where they come from, and how synthesizers approximate vowel sounds

Resonance as Spectral Shaping

A resonant cavity amplifies sounds whose wavelength fits inside it and attenuates everything else. This is a form of filtering: the cavity applies a frequency-dependent gain to the source sound. The frequencies it amplifies are called formants.

A guitar body is a resonant cavity. The air inside the body resonates at specific frequencies that depend on its volume and shape. When a string vibrates, harmonics that fall near these resonances are amplified; harmonics far from resonances are not. This is part of what gives different guitar body shapes their different timbral characters.

The human vocal tract is a resonant cavity that can be actively shaped by moving the tongue, lips, jaw, and velum (the soft palate). Different configurations produce different resonant frequencies, which is how we produce different vowel sounds from the same source (the vocal cords).

The Source-Filter Model of the Voice

The standard acoustics model of the voice separates two independent systems:

Source: The vocal cords vibrate, producing a periodic pulse train — a waveform rich in harmonics. The fundamental frequency of this vibration determines the pitch of the voice. The spectral shape of the source is roughly −12 dB/octave (harmonics fall off with 1/n² in amplitude, which is 6 dB per harmonic doubling frequency).

Filter: The vocal tract — a tube approximately 17 cm long for an adult male — shapes the source spectrum with its resonant frequencies (formants). The formants are determined entirely by the tract’s shape, independently of the vocal cord frequency.

This means pitch and timbre are independent in the voice: you can say “ee” at any pitch, and the formant structure that makes it “ee” remains constant. The vocal cord frequency changes with pitch; the vocal tract shape changes with vowel.

A synthesizer that models the voice uses a harmonic oscillator as the source and a filter bank as the vocal tract.

The pattern is clear:

  • F1 is controlled primarily by jaw height (open jaw = high F1, closed jaw = low F1)
  • F2 is controlled primarily by tongue position (front of mouth = high F2, back of mouth = low F2)

The consonants and higher formants (F3 and above) add detail and distinguish similar vowels and individual voice quality, but F1 and F2 carry most of the vowel identity information.

Why Resonance Frequencies Move

When you change vowel shape, the vocal tract length and cross-sectional area profile change. The resonant frequencies move because the physical dimensions of the resonator change.

For a simple cylindrical tube, the resonant frequencies are:

fₙ = (2n−1) × c / (4L)   (n = 1, 2, 3...)

This is the closed-tube formula (closed at the glottis end, open at the lips). Shorter tube (lips protruded more or jaw raised) → higher resonant frequencies. Longer tube → lower resonant frequencies.

The vocal tract is not a simple cylinder — it has constrictions and expansions. Each constriction raises some resonance frequencies and lowers others, depending on its position in the tract. A tongue constriction near the middle of the tract raises F2 if it is forward and lowers F2 if it is back. A constriction near the lips affects mainly F1.

Perturbation theory from acoustics (first developed for musical instrument acoustics) makes this precise: a constriction at a point of high velocity raises the resonant frequency, and a constriction at a point of high pressure lowers it. The pressure and velocity profiles of each mode determine which resonances shift and in what direction.

Formant Synthesis

A classic approach to voice synthesis is to model the vocal tract as a bank of bandpass filters — one per formant — and feed the harmonic source through them:

voice(t) = source(t) * [BP(F1) + BP(F2) + BP(F3)]

where BP(F) is a bandpass filter centered at formant frequency F.

The bandwidth of each bandpass filter is also important: a narrow bandwidth (high Q) produces a more resonant, nasal quality; a wide bandwidth (low Q) produces a more neutral tone. Natural vowel formants have bandwidths from about 50 Hz (narrow, nasal) to 200 Hz (wide, breathy).

Moving the formant frequencies over time produces vowel transitions and diphthongs. Spoken language consists largely of rapidly changing formant trajectories — it is the movement, not the static vowel positions, that carries much of the intelligibility.

Formant Synthesis vs. LPC

Linear Predictive Coding (LPC) is a related but different approach to speech synthesis that directly estimates the vocal tract’s filter coefficients from the speech signal. Rather than explicitly tracking formant frequencies, LPC fits an all-pole filter model to the spectral envelope of the speech. The result is the same kind of source-filter model but with the filter derived automatically from analysis rather than designed manually.

LPC synthesis was the technology behind the Texas Instruments Speak & Spell (1978) and many other early speech synthesis products. The characteristic “robotic” quality of early LPC synthesis came from using a simplified source model (a pure buzz or pure noise) rather than a realistic glottal source.

Connection to Synthesizer Design

Formant synthesis is directly realized in several synthesizers:

  • The EMS Vocoder (and later vocoder modules) analyze an input speech signal to extract formant envelope information, then apply it to a synthesizer sound
  • The formant filter bank in the design of this site is a set of bandpass filters whose center frequencies and bandwidths can be patched from CV to trace vowel trajectories
  • Karplus-Strong synthesis of body resonance (guitar, cello) uses the same principle: a resonant filter applied to a source signal

Understanding formants explains why vowel filters sound the way they do. When you sweep a bandpass filter across a harmonically rich waveform, you are emulating the effect of a vocal tract resonance sweeping in frequency — which is why it sounds voice-like.
